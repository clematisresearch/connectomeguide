# Writing a Guided Research Commentary (Introductory Level)

For the introductory level, you will be developing foundational skills in neuroscience research. Based on our guided exercise, you will write a research commentary of 500-1000 words summarizing your analyses, observations, and interpretations. 

# Writing a Research Manuscript (Advanced Level)

Writing a scientific report may feel unfamiliar if this is your first research project. Fortunately, many of the skills used by professional researchers can be learned through practice and guidance.

For the **Advanced Level** of both competition tracks, your written report will take the form of a research manuscript. We encourage you to communicate your research in a format similar to that used by scientists when sharing their work with the broader research community in a peer-reviewed journal. 

Below is a concise guide to writing a research paper produced by the **Taylor & Francis Group (2021)**, one of the world's largest academic publishing companies. The guide provides practical advice on structuring a scientific manuscript, as well as tips for communicating research clearly and effectively. For the competition, your written report will include an introduction, methods, results, discussion, conclusion, and abstract, and title.

# Word Count

The expected word count varies depending on the level of submission:

* **Introductory Level:** approximately **500–1,000 words**
* **Advanced Level:** approximately **2,000–4,000 words**

These ranges are intended as guidelines. There is no formal word limit for either level. However, scientific communication is most effective when it is concise and focused. We therefore recommend that participants **do not exceed 7,000 words**, unless there is a compelling reason to do so.

If you need to write an abstract (i.e., for advanced level submissions) we recommend that the **abstract be no longer than 350 words**.

# Formatting Requirements

There are no strict formatting requirements for written reports submitted to Connectome2026.

Participants are welcome to use any formatting style, provided that the report is **clear, readable, well-organized, and consistent throughout**.

That said, if you are unsure where to begin, the following conventions are commonly used in academic writing:

* Font: Times New Roman, Arial, or Calibri.
* Font size: 11–12 pt
* Line spacing: 1.5 or double-spaced
* Page margins: approximately 1 inch (2.54 cm) on all sides
* Page numbering: recommended for longer reports
* Section headings: clearly labelled and used consistently

For Advanced-Level submissions, we additionally recommend including clear section headings such as:

* Abstract
* Introduction
* Methods
* Results
* Discussion
* References

Ultimately, judges will evaluate the content of your work rather than the specific formatting choices you make. However, it is important that you communicate your ideas and findings clearly and professionally.

# References and Citation

Please make sure to appropriately acknowledge any research papers, datasets, software tools (e.g., **Neuroglancer** and **neuPrint**), figures, images, or other resources that you use in your report. Giving credit to the work of others is an important part of scientific research and allows readers to trace the sources of information, ideas, and methods used in your project.

If you are unfamiliar with academic referencing, we recommend reviewing the following guide:

**Newcastle University Academic Skills Kit: Referencing**
https://www.ncl.ac.uk/academic-skills-kit/good-academic-practice/referencing/

## What is Referencing?

In brief, referencing means acknowledging the sources of information that you have used in your work.

This is generally a two-step process:

### 1. In-Text Citation

When referring to information, ideas, methods, datasets, or findings from another source, include a citation within the text of your report.

For example:

> The complete adult *Drosophila* brain connectome was recently reconstructed using electron microscopy data (Scheffer et al., 2020).

In this example, **(Scheffer et al., 2020)** is an in-text citation. It identifies the authors and publication year of the source from which the information originated. You insert the citation within the sentence to indicate the source of the information. In this case, the citation refers to the study by Scheffer and colleagues in 2020 (`https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.57443`), and it is formatted in APA style as (Scheffer et al., 2020).

There are many citation styles, including **APA**, **Harvard**, **MLA**, and **Vancouver**. You are welcome to use any citation style, provided that you remain consistent throughout your report.

### 2. Reference List

At the end of your report, include a **References** section containing the full bibliographic information for every source cited in the text. For example, the full APA-style reference would be:

```{admonition} Example APA Reference
Scheffer, L. K., Xu, C. S., Januszewski, M., Lu, Z., Takemura, S. Y., Hayworth, K. J., Huang, G. B., Shinomiya, K., Maitin-Shepard, J., Berg, S., Clements, J., Hubbard, P., Katz, W. T., Umayam, L. A., Zhao, T., Ackerman, D., Blakely, T., Bogovic, J., Dolafi, T., ... Plaza, S. M. (2020). A connectome and analysis of the adult *Drosophila* central brain. *eLife, 9*, e57443. `https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.57443`
```

### What Should Be Referenced?

As a general rule, you should provide references for:

* Research papers and review articles
* Datasets
* Software tools and platforms (e.g., Neuroglancer, neuPrint)
* Figures, images, and visualizations that you did not create yourself
* Books, websites, and online resources
* Methods, concepts, or ideas that originate from another source

When in doubt, it is usually better to provide a citation than to omit one.

Proper referencing is a fundamental part of scientific integrity and helps ensure that credit is given to the researchers and communities whose work supports your own.

## Citing neuPrint and Neuroglancer

If you use **neuPrint** or **Neuroglancer** in your analyses, visualizations, or report, we encourage you to cite the corresponding resources.

### neuPrint

**APA Style Reference**

Plaza, S. M., Clements, J., Dolafi, T., Umayam, L., Neubarth, N. N., Scheffer, L. K., & Berg, S. (2022). neuPrint: An open access tool for EM connectomics. *Frontiers in Neuroinformatics, 16*, 896292. `https://doi.org/10.3389/fninf.2022.896292`

### Neuroglancer

**APA Style Reference**

Maitin-Shepard, J. (2020). *Neuroglancer* [Computer software]. https://github.com/google/neuroglancer

```{admonition} Example In-Text Citation
neuPrint has become a widely used platform for exploring connectomics datasets (Plaza et al., 2022). Similarly, Neuroglancer provides an interactive environment for visualizing large-scale volumetric neuroscience data (Maitin-Shepard, 2020).
```

We recognize that there are multiple ways to cite software tools. The examples above are provided as recommended citations for participants who use neuPrint or Neuroglancer in their projects.
